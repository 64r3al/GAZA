DOCUMENTED LIVES / REVIEWED 16 OCT 2026

A hard pill to swallow.

Childhood is supposed to be made of ordinary things: a familiar voice, a schoolbook, a bicycle, a safe place to sleep. Published accounts from Gaza describe children whose ordinary world has been broken apart—not as a metaphor, but as daily life.

THE PEOPLE INSIDE THE OCTOBER REPORT

The year has a statistic. Each family has a name.

The following accounts were summarized by UNICEF on 7 October 2026. These are specific reported lives, not invented composites and not a claim that every child has the same experience.

HOME / FAMILY LOSS / CHILD LABOUR

Jamal and Hamza: collecting waste instead of returning to school.

UNICEF reported that Jamal and Hamza lost their home and their brother during the war. They now live with nine other family members in a damaged tent and spend their days collecting waste to earn money and help support the family. Their circumstances show how a ceasefire does not, by itself, restore housing, income, safety or a normal school day.

SIBLINGS / BEREAVEMENT / CARE

Norhan, 15, is caring for her one-year-old sister.

In the same 7 October 2026 report, UNICEF described Norhan caring for her younger sister Narjis, who was born prematurely after their mother was killed during pregnancy. The account describes a teenager carrying caregiving responsibilities after a devastating family loss. No composite dialogue has been added here; this summary follows the published report.

ACCOUNT / 01 UNICEF · 09 OCT 2025

CARE / LOSS / CHILDHOOD

The child who became the caretaker.

Samar was 17 when UNICEF published her account in October 2025. Her father had died before the war; her mother went missing after a strike hit the family in December 2023. Samar took responsibility for her five younger siblings. UNICEF's reporting described the children being injured, the difficulty of finding medicine and bandages, and Samar trying to care for them while carrying injuries of her own.

“My little brothers and sisters now call me ‘Mama’.”

A child can need protection and be forced to provide it at the same time. That is part of what this account asks us to see.

ACCOUNT / 02 UNICEF · 26 MAR 2025

HOME / MEMORY / EVERYDAY THINGS

A bicycle in the rubble.

Qusay was 10 when UNICEF reported his family's return to northern Gaza in January 2025. The family had been displaced twice. While away, he kept thinking about his bicycle. When they returned, their home had burned; the bicycle, his books, toys, clothes and bed were gone. The details are small only if we forget that these are the ordinary things a childhood is made from.

“I kept thinking about my bicycle.”
Read Qusay's original account at UNICEF ↗
ACCOUNT / 03 UNICEF · 25 JAN 2024

SHELTER / SANITATION / PRIVACY

When even a bathroom queue becomes a daily ordeal.

In a UNICEF account published in January 2024, Aya described how her family fled their home and spent time sleeping on the street before finding shelter at Al-Aqsa University. Families there borrowed blankets and mattresses from one another; people shared sleeping space because there was so little. Access to a bathroom could mean a wait of around two hours. This is a dated snapshot of the conditions reported at that time—not a claim that one particular shelter has the same conditions today.

“There’s a long line — two hours maybe.”

ACCOUNT / 04 UNICEF · 27 JAN 2026

EDUCATION / COLD / DISPLACEMENT

A major exam in a cold, wet tent.

Jana was 17 when UNICEF described her effort to prepare for the tawjihi, a high-school matriculation exam. Displaced from northern Gaza to Deir al-Balah, she studied on the floor of a tent that was dark, cold, wet, and regularly flooded. Books cost too much, so she borrowed them and tried to keep them dry. The family relied on a local soup kitchen for food. Her aim remained simple: study, feel safe, and have a chance at an ordinary future.

“I need to study, but there is no light; it’s cold and wet.”

ACCOUNT / 05 UNICEF · 15 APR 2025

RUBBLE / FAMILY LOSS / PLAY

The search for toys in a destroyed home.

Reda, 12, and Siraj, 5, had been repeatedly displaced with their family. UNICEF reported in April 2025 that their father and older brother had been killed in a drone attack. When the family returned to Jabaliya refugee camp, their home had only one wall still standing. The boys recovered a few plastic toys and tried to play near the ruins, while also helping their family fetch water. The smallness of the toys is not the point: it is what childhood tries to keep hold of after everything else has changed.

“I started searching for what remained of my toys and belongings.”

ACCOUNT / 06 UNICEF · 25 JAN 2024

INJURY / ACCESSIBILITY / ISOLATION

After the rubble, a life without room to move.

UNICEF's January 2024 account described Mays, a girl using a wheelchair because of a leg injury after being pulled from under rubble. She was living in a shelter in Khan Yunis's industrial zone. Before the war, she had loved the sea, her phone, friends, and games. In the shelter, there was little room for activity; messaging friends was one of the ways she tried to find happiness. A physical injury can change how a child moves through the world, while displacement can take away the space needed to recover.

“There’s no space to do anything else.”

ACCOUNT / 07 UNICEF · 22 MAR 2025

WATER / SCHOOL / REPEATED DISPLACEMENT

When finding water becomes the day's work.

Mais Abu Saif told UNICEF that her family fled Jabaliya, moved to Rafah, then fled again to Khan Yunis. Her schooling stopped and daily life narrowed to a tent, finding water and collecting firewood. After the family returned to the place where their home had stood, she said there was little left and she spent her time searching for water and helping her family. The report, published in March 2025, also described severe damage to water and sanitation infrastructure; water shortages made washing, cooking, and caring for injured relatives much harder.

“I lost everything — my school — and my life was confined to a tent.”

WHAT THE NUMBERS MISS / 04

There is a life behind every line.

A report can count a displaced family. It cannot fully measure what it means for a child to lose the room they knew, to study without a stable classroom, to wait for a missing parent, or to take responsibility for younger siblings while needing care themselves. Those details belong to the people who lived them—and to the reporting that records their words.
