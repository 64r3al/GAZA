DOCUMENTED LIVES / REVIEWED 16 OCT 2026

A hard pill to swallow.

Childhood is supposed to be made of ordinary things: a familiar voice, a schoolbook, a bicycle, a safe place to sleep. Published accounts from Gaza describe children whose ordinary world has been broken apart—not as a metaphor, but as daily life.

BEFORE YOU READ / 00

These are real accounts, not invented characters.

The personal accounts on this page were published by UNICEF. The wider conditions are drawn from dated United Nations reporting. Each section links to its original source. Names, ages, quotations and figures are presented in the context and timeframe reported; the figures do not all refer to the same reporting period.

ACCOUNT / 01 UNICEF · 09 OCT 2025

CARE / LOSS / CHILDHOOD

The child who became the caretaker.

Samar was 17 when UNICEF published her account in October 2025. Her father had died before the war; her mother went missing after a strike hit the family in December 2023. Samar took responsibility for her five younger siblings. UNICEF's reporting described the children being injured, the difficulty of finding medicine and bandages, and Samar trying to care for them while carrying injuries of her own.

“My little brothers and sisters now call me ‘Mama’.”

A child can need protection and be forced to provide it at the same time. That is part of what this account asks us to see.

ACCOUNT / 02 UNICEF · 26 MAR 2025

HOME / MEMORY / EVERYDAY THINGS

A bicycle in the rubble.

Qusay was 10 when UNICEF reported his family's return to northern Gaza in January 2025. The family had been displaced twice. While away, he kept thinking about his bicycle. When they returned, their home had burned; the bicycle, his books, toys, clothes and bed were gone. The details are small only if we forget that these are the ordinary things a childhood is made from.

“I kept thinking about my bicycle.”

WHAT THE NUMBERS MISS / 04

There is a life behind every line.

A report can count a displaced family. It cannot fully measure what it means for a child to lose the room they knew, to study without a stable classroom, to wait for a missing parent, or to take responsibility for younger siblings while needing care themselves. Those details belong to the people who lived them—and to the reporting that records their words.
